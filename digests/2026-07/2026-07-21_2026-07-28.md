### 1. The Human-AI Substitution Principle: When will you be replaced by AI in your organization?

- [ ] **APPROVE FOR SOCIAL** (pillar: `AI & Jobs`)

- **Source:** arXiv: Computers & Society (cs.CY)
- **Link:** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20781>
- **Published:** Jul 27, 2026
- **Author(s):** Bonny Banerjee, Shreya Singh
- **Keyword score:** 30.04 (AI strong: 15, AI booster: 2, domain: 14)
- **Pillar:** AI & Jobs  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 82/100
- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: high · policy makers: high · general public: medium

**Summary (Claude):**

> When does AI replace a human worker, and under what organizational conditions? This paper presents a formal model of Human-AI Task Allocation (HAT) in hierarchical organizations, deriving a precise 'substitution principle' based on the economic asymmetry between human skill acquisition (costly and slow) and AI capability scaling (cheaper and faster). The model predicts that AI adoption can produce abrupt workforce transitions rather than gradual ones, that middle-management roles face elevated vulnerability, and that highly skilled workers are protected only up to a threshold shaped by organizational depth and risk differentials. The analysis also suggests AI flattens hierarchies and widens managerial spans of control. The paper is a working paper from arXiv and has not yet undergone peer review.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper derives formal conditions under which AI substitutes for human labor across organizational layers, finding that middle management faces structural — not merely incidental — vulnerability, and that even highly skilled workers lose protection once AI capability crosses a threshold shaped by organizational depth. From CAMO's perspective on AI and jobs, the result complements our own work on job design: the 'bad job economy' paper (Toward a Bad Job Economy) shows a parallel mechanism whereby AI lowers the cost of satisfactory performance and induces firms to replace high-effort roles, and the HAT model here offers a complementary structural account of where those replacements concentrate in a hierarchy. Together, both lines of work suggest that the workforce risk from AI is not uniform across levels — it is shaped by organizational architecture, cost asymmetries, and incentive design simultaneously.

**Matched CAMO research:**

- [Toward a Bad Job Economy: AI Adoption, Agency Costs, and Job Design](https://camo.hku.hk/toward-a-bad-job-economy-ai-adoption-agency-costs-and-job-design/) — Both papers model how AI's cost asymmetry relative to human labor reshapes which roles firms sustain, with overlapping predictions about downward pressure on wages, job quality, and the conditions under which AI substitutes rather than complements workers.

**Key takeaway:** AI replacement in organizations is not random — it concentrates in middle management and follows a predictable structural logic tied to cost asymmetries, organizational depth, and risk, meaning firms can anticipate vulnerability hot-spots rather than react to them.

Each floor represents a hierarchical layer, with the middle floors visually thinner and structurally stressed, as if under compression. **Summary (Claude):**

> As large language models become standard tools for task execution and decision-making at work, researchers have developed and validated a Spanish-language psychometric scale to measure psychological dependency on LLMs. The LLM-D12-SP distinguishes two forms of dependency: instrumental (relying on LLMs to perform tasks and inform decisions) and relational (turning to LLMs for companionship and social interaction). Tested on 386 Spanish-speaking participants, the scale showed strong internal consistency and confirmed that both dependency dimensions correlate with internet addiction and perceived trustworthiness of LLMs, but not with need for cognition. This cross-linguistic validation extends prior English and Arabic versions, offering organizations a standardized tool to diagnose how deeply, and in what ways, employees are psychologically anchored to LLM tools.

**CAMO angle:**

> This study introduces a validated instrument for measuring two distinct forms of LLM dependency—task-oriented reliance and social-psychological reliance—among Spanish-speaking users. From CAMO's perspective on AI adoption, the more practically urgent finding is that instrumental dependency (using LLMs as a decision-making crutch) is now measurable at the individual level, which opens the door to assessing whether organizational AI adoption is building genuine capability or deepening unhealthy over-reliance. The scale's weak correlation with need for cognition is worth noting: employees who depend most on LLMs are not simply those who prefer not to think hard, suggesting dependency is shaped more by workflow design and perceived tool trustworthiness than by individual cognitive disposition.

**Key takeaway:** Organizations now have a cross-linguistically validated tool to distinguish whether employees depend on LLMs as a decision aid or as a social substitute—a meaningful difference for workforce design and AI governance.

The finding reframes the obstacle to enforceable stranding regulation: it is not a forecasting problem but a measurable, jurisdiction-specific reporting gap that targeted disclosure mandates—monitored by tools like BeDA—could systematically close.

**CAMO angle:**

> This study deploys a multimodal LLM as a large-scale compliance auditing instrument—evaluating thousands of corporate disclosures against a regulatory standard rather than relying on human reviewers. From CAMO's perspective on algorithmic decision support, the work illustrates both the promise and the limits of AI-assisted measurement: BeDA achieves high inter-extractor agreement and convergent validity, but it surfaces a data-quality crisis that no algorithm can resolve on its own. The deeper implication is that AI tools for regulatory compliance are only as useful as the disclosure regimes that feed them—making the design of reporting mandates an organisational and governance question, not just a technical one.

**Key takeaway:** AI can audit corporate climate disclosures at scale, but four out of five building-sector reports still lack the basic metric—carbon intensity per square metre—that stranding regulation requires, making the reporting gap itself the primary policy problem.
