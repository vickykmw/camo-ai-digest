> Abstract: How should people choose careers when artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming the nature of work? We first compare six recent projections of occupational exposure to task automation with AI, examining their methods and assumptions. We then propose a new empirical model of occupational AI exposure based on 2025 query data from Anthropic and OpenAI. We find marked heterogeneity in model predictions, though models published since 2020 show positive relationships among AI exposure, salaries, and occupational complexity. **Summary (Claude):**

> When AI capital becomes cheaper, does it create formal jobs or push workers into informality? This paper builds a dynamic general equilibrium model calibrated to a developing economy with a large informal sector and finds that the answer hinges entirely on one parameter: whether AI substitutes for or complements formal workers. Under substitution, falling AI prices erode formal labor demand and expand the informal buffer. Under complementarity, they raise formal employment, wages, and output. The core contribution is showing that the same price shock can produce diametrically opposite distributional outcomes depending on the structural relationship between AI and labor.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper argues that cheaper AI is neither inherently good nor bad for formal employment in developing economies — the outcome is determined by the elasticity of substitution between AI capital and human labor, a parameter that varies by sector, task structure, and institutional context. From CAMO's perspective on how AI reshapes career paths and employment structures, this framing reinforces a point implicit in our research on job design: AI's labor-market effect is an organizational and contractual question as much as a technological one. The concern raised in our 'Toward a Bad Job Economy' working paper — that AI disproportionately lowers the cost of satisfactory rather than excellent performance — is compounded in developing-economy settings where informal employment already absorbs displaced formal workers, creating a structural floor beneath which wage pressure can accumulate silently.
