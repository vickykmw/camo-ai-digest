**Summary (Claude):**

> Most Fortune 500 companies claim to govern their AI, yet few leaders can name who is actually responsible for shutting down a harmful model. The article, written by Adobe's director of AI governance, argues that the operative question in AI governance is not 'are we governing AI?' but 'who has the authority and obligation to act when something goes wrong?' Without a clear accountability owner, governance frameworks become procedural theater — policies exist on paper while no individual bears real consequence for inaction. The piece makes the case that accountability assignment, not policy documentation, is the foundational design problem in enterprise AI governance.

**CAMO angle:**

> This article argues that AI governance fails not from a lack of policy but from unresolved accountability — no one owns the decision to stop a harmful model. From CAMO's perspective on AI adoption inside organizations, this maps directly onto a structural problem: firms race to deploy AI while leaving authority structures ambiguous. CAMO's Deloitte-HKU AI Adoption Index found that the dominant barriers to successful AI adoption are organizational and executional rather than technical, and the absence of a named accountability owner is precisely the kind of executional gap that converts promising deployments into liability exposure. The article's framing complements CAMO's broader finding that closing the gap between AI promise and organizational performance requires deliberate redesign of roles and decision rights, not just investment in tools.

**Key takeaway:** AI governance is only as real as the named individual who can order a model shut down.
