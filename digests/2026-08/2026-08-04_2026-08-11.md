### 1. Learning by Chatting? Investigating the Impact of Generative AI on Information Seeking and Learning

- [ ] **APPROVE FOR SOCIAL** (pillar: `AI Adoption`)

- **Source:** arXiv: Computers & Society (cs.CY)
- **Link:** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11669>
- **Published:** Aug 10, 2026
- **Author(s):** Shravika Mittal, Su Lin Blodgett, Q. Vera Liao
- **Keyword score:** 15.52 (AI strong: 15, AI booster: 0, domain: 1)
- **Pillar:** AI Adoption  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 72/100
- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: high · policy makers: high · general public: medium

**Summary (Claude):**

> A field experiment assigned participants to use either ChatGPT or Google Search for informal learning over eight days. Those using ChatGPT offloaded information selection to the AI, which reduced their sense of agency and increased meta-cognitive load. The study identifies two distortions: ChatGPT outputs skew toward solution-oriented answers rather than principled knowledge, and the conversational interface narrows users' exploration of the broader knowledge space. On average, ChatGPT users had worse learning outcomes than Google users, with the gap largest for higher-order critical thinking. The findings suggest a structural tension between AI-assisted information seeking and the kind of active cognitive engagement that produces durable learning.

**CAMO angle:**

> This study finds that delegating information seeking to generative AI can degrade learning outcomes, particularly for higher-order cognition, because the conversational interface reduces user agency and narrows knowledge exploration. From CAMO's perspective on AI adoption inside organizations, this is a significant design and deployment concern: firms rolling out GenAI tools for knowledge work may inadvertently erode the skill development and judgment that make experienced employees valuable in the first place. CAMO's work on the mediocrity trap in GenAI (Better Technology, Worse Motivation) offers a complementary lens — where that paper documents reduced effort incentives, this study documents a parallel cognitive mechanism through which AI assistance can quietly lower the ceiling on human performance.

**Matched CAMO research:**

**Key takeaway:** Using ChatGPT for information seeking can produce worse learning outcomes than traditional search, particularly for critical thinking, because the tool's conversational design encourages cognitive offloading rather than active knowledge construction.

**Summary (Claude):**

> As large language models move from pilot deployments into enterprise production, the tools meant to manage AI risks remain poorly mapped to the governance frameworks organizations actually use. This paper audits 21 prominent open-source LLM evaluation and security tools against a 32-subcategory risk taxonomy, using an LLM-assisted retrieval pipeline to extract each tool's capabilities. The findings reveal a pronounced imbalance: tooling clusters heavily around technical and operational controls — adversarial testing, runtime guardrails, model evaluation — while governance, legal and regulatory, and financial risk categories are largely unaddressed. The authors argue this gap motivates a layered architecture that combines automated tool-based controls with organizational and regulatory processes. Human oversight remains necessary precisely where the tools are weakest.

**CAMO angle:**

From CAMO's perspective on the statistical and algorithmic foundations of trustworthy AI-driven decisions, the finding matters because it exposes a structural asymmetry: the machinery for catching prompt injections and model failures is far more developed than the machinery for accountability, legal compliance, or financial risk — exactly the controls that C-suite decisions depend on. Organizations scaling from pilot to production should not assume that deploying evaluation tooling is equivalent to deploying governance.

### 4. Better Together: Quantifying the Benefits of AI-Assisted Recruitment

**Summary (Claude):**

> Can AI-conducted structured interviews improve hiring accuracy beyond what resumes alone reveal? Two field experiments at a live recruitment platform suggest yes: candidates shortlisted using an AI Interview Report passed subsequent blind human interviews at rates 17–20 percentage points higher than those shortlisted from resumes only. The gains are largest for junior candidates, where resumes carry the least signal—adding AI ratings raised out-of-sample AUC by 0.18 versus 0.08 for more experienced candidates. The study positions AI interviews not as a replacement for human judgment but as an information-generation tool that moves screening costs from firms to applicants.

**CAMO angle:**

> This study argues that LLM-based structured interviews generate genuinely new candidate information—rather than simply reranking existing signals—and that this information most benefits assessments of junior workers whose resumes are least diagnostic. From CAMO's perspective on AI and jobs, the finding raises an underexamined distributional question: who bears the cost of AI-assisted screening? The 75% non-completion rate means the efficiency gains for firms are partly funded by a burden placed on applicants, and that burden may fall unevenly across job-seekers. CAMO's work on the hidden social penalties of AI use at work (see 'Research: The Hidden Penalty of Using AI at Work') suggests that how AI is embedded in hiring processes shapes not only selection accuracy but also the career prospects and perceived competence of those who engage with it.

**Key takeaway:** AI-conducted structured interviews meaningfully improve shortlisting accuracy—especially for junior candidates—but shift the screening burden onto applicants, 75% of whom drop out before completion.

**Summary (Claude):**

> When measuring how much a text is 'about' a concept — say, ESG themes in financial documents — standard methods count words or compare embeddings. This paper asks whether the internal activations of a frozen, unmodified large language model carry richer concept information than its own stated output. Using linear probes and the Recursive Feature Machine algorithm applied to an annotated ESG dataset, the authors find that the best linear probe matches a fine-tuned classifier's accuracy to within 0.6 percentage points and outperforms the model's own direct answer in eleven of twelve comparisons. The implication is that LLMs 'know' more about concept content than they express — and that this internal signal can be tapped without costly fine-tuning, offering a more principled basis for automated text classification in applied domains.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper demonstrates that the gap between what an LLM knows internally and what it reports externally is not merely a philosophical curiosity — it is a measurable, exploitable signal with practical consequences for automated text analysis. From CAMO's perspective on AI Algorithms & Data, the finding raises a pointed question for practitioners relying on LLM-generated labels in domains like ESG reporting or performance evaluation: if the model's own stated answer is routinely outperformed by its activations, then pipelines built on model outputs alone may be systematically losing information. This connects to the broader concern, visible in CAMO's work on AI-driven performance evaluation at Dingdong, that the accuracy and legitimacy of AI-generated attributions depend critically on the right extraction method — not just the right model.

- **Pillar:** AI Algorithms & Data  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 72/100

**Summary (Claude):**

> When competing firms use bidding algorithms supplied by the same third-party provider, their bids synchronise in ways that effectively coordinate market behaviour without explicit collusion. Studying five-minute battery bids in Australia's electricity market, the paper finds that batteries sharing an autobidding provider forgo profitable dispatch during evening peaks to protect the revenues of same-provider rivals.
