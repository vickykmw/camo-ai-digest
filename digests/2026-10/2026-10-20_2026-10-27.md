**Summary (Claude):**

The study uses two quasi-experimental designs to establish causal estimates, making it methodologically stronger than most observational AI-at-work research. The core implication: AI-generated outputs require interpretation and social validation, which drives people toward colleagues rather than away from them, though the pattern of those relationships shifts in structure.

### 3. When AI Gets Cheaper, Do Prices Follow? Information Delays and Algorithmic Pricing

- **Pillar:** AI Algorithms & Data  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 62/100
- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: medium · policy makers: high · general public: medium

**Summary (Claude):**

> When AI inference costs fall, do consumers see lower prices? This simulation study uses two competing sellers, logit demand, and Q-learning agents to test how information delays and repricing constraints shape how much of a cost reduction reaches buyers. The short answer: not much, and not reliably. Even under the most favorable conditions — fresh rival-price data, frequent repricing — the two firms passed on only 55.6% and 44.0% of the cost reduction respectively. Consumer surplus under learned pricing policies sat below the static Nash benchmark in all 16 experimental conditions. Crucially, the interaction between information delay and repricing frequency on consumer surplus was statistically indistinguishable from zero, suggesting these structural features are not the primary lever explaining underpassthrough.

**CAMO angle:**

> This simulation study asks whether algorithmic pricing agents — trained via Q-learning in a duopoly — pass cost savings to consumers, or pocket them. From CAMO's perspective on AI algorithms and data, the result is a reminder that learned policies optimized for firm-level reward can diverge systematically from socially efficient outcomes, even absent explicit coordination. The gap between the learned equilibrium and the static Nash benchmark across all 16 conditions points to a design problem: when the objective function and the training environment are specified by the firms deploying these algorithms, consumer welfare is not guaranteed to follow. This matters for regulators and platform designers who assume competitive markets will transmit AI efficiency gains downstream.

**Matched CAMO research:**

- [Seesaw Experimentation: A/B Tests with Spillovers](https://camo.hku.hk/2024-03-seesaw-experimentation-ab/) — Both papers examine how algorithmic optimization on a measured primary dimension (revenue/price) can produce outcomes that diverge from broader welfare goals, and both use simulation-style experimental designs to identify the conditions under which this divergence occurs.

**Key takeaway:** Algorithmic pricing agents trained to maximize firm payoffs may retain a large share of AI cost savings rather than passing them to consumers, even in competitive markets with frequent repricing.

The core finding: the procedural method governing a judge matters at least as much as which AI model is chosen — and most current AI evaluation research never tests the procedure.

**CAMO angle:**

From CAMO's perspective on algorithmic foundations for trustworthy AI-driven decisions, this is a structural warning for any organization using AI to score employee performance, customer interactions, or compliance outputs. CAMO's work on AI-driven performance evaluation — including the Dingdong VOC case, where AI tagging accuracy of 92% still required an appeals process and managerial discretion — illustrates exactly why the procedural architecture around an AI judge shapes outcomes as much as the model itself.
