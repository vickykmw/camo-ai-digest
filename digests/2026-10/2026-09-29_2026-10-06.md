### 1. Artificial Intelligence and the New Science of Culture

- [ ] **APPROVE FOR SOCIAL** (pillar: `AI Algorithms & Data`)

- **Source:** arXiv: Computers & Society (cs.CY)
- **Link:** <https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06240>
- **Published:** Oct 06, 2026
- **Author(s):** Douglas R. Guilbeault, Bhargav Srinivasa Desikan, James A. Evans
- **Keyword score:** 23.97 (AI strong: 8, AI booster: 0, domain: 16)
- **Pillar:** AI Algorithms & Data  ·  **Pillar relevance:** 58/100
- **Audience fit:** managers/C-suite: low · policy makers: medium · general public: medium

**Summary (Claude):**

> What happens to culture when the tools we use to describe it also generate it? This theoretical paper argues that cognition and culture are not separate phenomena but mutually constitutive: individual thought shapes collective meaning, and collective meaning shapes individual thought. Machine learning methods—embedding models and large generative AI—now make this loop empirically measurable for the first time. The authors use vector geometry to trace conceptual structures across individuals, organizations, and societies, and show that cultural dynamics can predict simultaneous independent discoveries across distant researchers. They then formalize a core risk: as generative AI becomes both the instrument for measuring culture and an active participant in producing it, a 'Heisenberg-like' uncertainty principle emerges—the more precisely AI fixes a culture's current state, the less predictable its trajectory becomes. The paper closes with ethical guidelines on AI-mediated cultural drift and human cultural agency.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper argues that generative AI has crossed a qualitative threshold: it is no longer merely a tool for analyzing culture but an agent that co-produces it, introducing a reflexivity that makes cultural trajectories fundamentally harder to predict. From CAMO's perspective on algorithmic foundations and trustworthy AI-driven decisions, the implication is sobering—when the measurement instrument and the phenomenon under study are the same system, standard assumptions about stable data-generating processes break down. Organizations and policymakers relying on AI-driven cultural or sentiment analytics should be alert to the possibility that the models informing their decisions are simultaneously reshaping the behavior those models purport to measure.

**Matched CAMO research:**

- [Seesaw Experimentation: A/B Tests with Spillovers](https://camo.hku.hk/2024-03-seesaw-experimentation-ab/) — Both papers grapple with measurement interventions that alter the system being measured—seesaw experimentation shows how optimizing a measured dimension degrades unmeasured ones, which is structurally analogous to the paper's Heisenberg-like principle that fixing a culture's position degrades trajectory prediction.

**Key takeaway:** Generative AI does not just measure culture—it actively reshapes it, making cultural trajectories less predictable the more precisely AI tries to fix them.

**Summary (Claude):**

> When AI automates work, the structure of the workflow matters as much as which individual steps AI can do well. This paper shows that AI tends to execute contiguous 'chains' of steps rather than isolated tasks — and this chaining logic can override simple comparative advantage: a step where humans outperform AI may still be handed to AI because it sits inside a chain of AI-executed steps. The implication is that automation exposure is not just a property of individual tasks but of their neighbours in a sequence. Empirically, steps adjacent to AI-executed steps are themselves more likely to be AI-executed, and gains from improving AI quality can be non-monotone, resembling a productivity J-curve where things get worse before they get better.

**CAMO angle:**

> This paper argues that AI automation spreads through workflows via contiguous chaining, not task-by-task substitution — meaning the productivity impact of AI depends heavily on how AI-exposed steps are spatially arranged inside a production process, not merely how many there are. From CAMO's perspective on AI adoption, the implication is that firms designing AI rollouts around individual task exposure scores are likely to misread both the speed and distribution of automation gains. CAMO's case evidence from Decathlon China, where AI optimised individual coding tasks but left coordination bottlenecks untouched, illustrates the same structural point from the practitioner side: the gains live in the system architecture, not the task list.

**Matched CAMO research:**

- [Compressing Coordination with AI at Decathlon China](https://camo.hku.hk/nanocase-compressingcoordinationwithaiatdecathlonchina/) — Both the paper and the Decathlon case converge on the finding that AI's productivity impact is determined by workflow structure and inter-task dependencies rather than the automation of isolated steps.
- [Toward a Bad Job Economy: AI Adoption, Agency Costs, and Job Design](https://camo.hku.hk/toward-a-bad-job-economy-ai-adoption-agency-costs-and-job-design/) — The chaining model's implication that human-advantaged steps can be reassigned to AI connects directly to CAMO's analysis of how AI reshapes job boundaries and which tasks remain with workers under cost-minimising firm behaviour.

**Key takeaway:** Where AI-executable steps cluster in a workflow determines automation outcomes far more than a simple count of how many tasks AI can perform.

The findings on prompt-injection vulnerability are particularly instructive: even well-designed aggregation pipelines can be gamed by adversarial inputs, echoing the self-fulfilling and reinforcement biases that CAMO's own algorithmic work has documented in commercial decision systems. The democratic deliberation setting is novel, but the underlying question — under what conditions can an AI system faithfully aggregate heterogeneous human inputs without systematic distortion — is one any organisation deploying AI for preference elicitation or collective sensemaking should take seriously.

**Summary (Claude):**

> When a large language model says it would take a risk, does that prediction match what it actually does? This study moves beyond black-box prompting to examine the internal representations driving self-reported and behaviorally enacted risk-taking in LLMs. Using activation steering across nine vector-extraction methods and four open-weight models, researchers find that self-report and behavior are governed by near-orthogonal internal directions — meaning the two channels are largely independent. Task-specific directives weakly reach both, but removing surface confounders strips away most of that behavioral effect. Critically, composing one behavior-moving and one self-report-moving direction can deliberately set the two in opposition, producing models that report low risk while acting high-risk (or vice versa) in 69–89% of cases. The finding reframes the self-report–behavior gap as a structural, inspectable representational property rather than a prompting artifact.
